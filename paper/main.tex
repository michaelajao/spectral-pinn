\begin{abstract}
Wang et al.~\cite{wang2026cnpinn} improve physics-informed neural network
accuracy by factorising each hidden weight at initialisation, training the
left factor and the singular values in place of the weight, and penalising
departures of the left factor from orthogonality with a hand-tuned weight
$\wU$. Their account assigns the gain to control over the weight spectrum. We
reproduce their advection case over 20 seeds and then measure the trained
factors, which their paper does not report. At the published
$\wU = 1/35000$ the trained left factor is not close to orthogonal ---
$\|U^\top U - I\|_2$ has median $0.98$, indicating a collapsed
eigendirection --- and the trained singular values differ from the effective
weight's actual singular values by up to a factor of 13. Constraining the
factor to be exactly orthogonal, which removes $\wU$ entirely, reaches only
$1.7\times10^{-2}$ median relative error against the penalty's
$8.4\times10^{-3}$; removing the penalty from the same parameterisation
returns the error to $2.1\times10^{-1}$, worse than an unconstrained
network's $8.4\times10^{-2}$. Reading the term as a bound on the factor's
scale predicts that a weaker bound should also work; we test that by pinning
only $\sigma_{\max}(U)$, and it is the worst regime of the six at
$3.7\times10^{-1}$ median, so what the penalty supplies is a pull of the whole
spectrum toward 1 rather than a bound on scale. Transferring the comparison to
two-dimensional shallow-water dam breaks, where the solution carries a shock
and the bounded-gradient hypothesis of the source paper's Theorem 2.1 fails,
no spectral regime separates from an unmodified network by more than about
10\% in $L^1$ depth error on the three shock cases, and $\wU$ can be moved
three decades without measurable effect. On the one smooth initial condition
in the set, exact orthogonality and spectrum-only training are a factor of
$2.5$ worse than an unmodified network. Establishing this required checking
our finite-volume reference against an independently implemented solver
family, which disagrees with it by more than the differences under test on
two of three benchmarks --- a floor that comparisons of this kind do not
usually report.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A physics-informed neural network represents the solution of a PDE by a network
and trains it by minimising the residual of the equation at sampled collocation
points, together with terms for the initial and boundary
data~\cite{raissi2019pinn}. The appeal is that the same machinery handles
forward and inverse problems, irregular geometries and partial data without a
mesh. The difficulty, visible from the earliest applications, is that training
often stalls on solutions with steep gradients or high-frequency content --- the
regime where classical solvers are also hardest, and therefore the regime that
would most justify the method.

Several distinct explanations have been offered for that failure and each comes
with its own remedy. Networks trained by gradient descent fit low frequencies
before high ones~\cite{rahaman2019spectral}, which motivates input encodings
that raise the frequency content the network can express cheaply: random Fourier
features~\cite{tancik2020fourier} and periodic
activations~\cite{sitzmann2020siren} are the standard instruments, and adaptive
activation slopes are a lighter variant~\cite{jagtap2020adaptive}. A second
account locates the problem in the loss rather than the architecture: residual
and boundary terms have gradients of very different magnitude, so one term
dominates and training converges to a solution that satisfies the wrong
constraint. That reading has produced gradient-based
rebalancing~\cite{wang2021gradient}, neural-tangent-kernel
weighting~\cite{wang2022ntk}, self-adaptive pointwise
weights~\cite{mcclenny2023selfadaptive} and augmented-Lagrangian treatments of
the boundary conditions~\cite{son2023alpinn}. A third account, the one this
paper is about, looks at the weights themselves.

Wang et al.~\cite{wang2026cnpinn} propose a diagnostic and a remedy built on a
quantity they call the condition number of the network,
$\kappa(f) = \|J(f)\|_F$. The name invites confusion --- this is an absolute
condition number, an $H^1$ seminorm of the gradient magnitude, and not the
$\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ of numerical linear algebra --- but the
construction is concrete. Each hidden weight is factorised once at
initialisation as $W_j = U_j S_j V_j^\top$; $V_j$ is frozen; $U_j$ and
$\mathrm{diag}(S_j)$ are trained in place of $W_j$; and the loss gains
\begin{equation}
  \wU \sum_j \DU_j^2, \qquad \DU_j = \|U_j^\top U_j - I\|_2
  \label{eq:penalty}
\end{equation}
so that $U_j$ is pushed toward orthogonality and $\mathrm{diag}(S_j)$ therefore
carries the singular values of $W_j$. The stated mechanism is that the optimiser
gains direct control of each layer's spectrum, which sets the Lipschitz scale
the network can express, and that this is what helps on steep targets. Across
five PDEs the method improves on a vanilla network by roughly an order of
magnitude.

Their conclusion lists three open problems, and they are the starting point
here. First, $\wU$ is tuned per equation across two and a half decades, from
$1/600$ to $1/200000$, and their Section 4.6.3 reports that NTK- and
DB-PINN-style adaptive weighting diverges on this particular term because the
penalty begins at exactly zero, so the adapted weight grows without bound.
Second, the penalty costs 1.28 to 1.83 times vanilla training time across their
width and collocation sweeps. Third, all five validation problems are, in their
own words, controlled synthetic examples, and all five are smooth.

The third point has a consequence the paper does not draw. Every hypothesis of
their Theorem 2.1 requires $u \in H^1$ with $\|J(u)\|_F$ bounded. A dam-break
solution carries a shock, so $\|J(u)\|_F$ is unbounded and $u \notin H^1$: the
theorem is formally inapplicable to the whole problem class, and the diagnostic
that motivates the method is a quantity that does not exist there. The authors
come close to saying so in another context, noting that the condition is
violated once training fully collapses. Whether the \emph{method} still helps when
its \emph{theory} does not apply is an empirical question, and it is the one that
makes shock-dominated flow the informative test rather than merely a harder one.

The apparatus for that test comes from a companion study by an overlapping
author group, submitted to \emph{Computers \& Fluids}~\cite{dambreak2026comparative}.
That study compares classical finite-volume schemes against several PINN
formulations on six two-dimensional dam-break initial conditions under one set
of conventions, and reports that a physics-only, finite-volume-informed network
fails on the short cases where a strong-form network merely does poorly. We
reuse its benchmark definitions, its conventions, and its differentiable HLLC
solver, and we inherit its finding about the finite-volume-informed baseline
rather than rederiving it. Two things follow. The comparison here is a
controlled substitution --- only the weight parameterisation changes between
entries, on fixed benchmarks and a fixed evaluation protocol --- and the
reference solver output produced for that study, computed by independent
implementations of HLL, Lax--Wendroff and MUSCL--Rusanov, is available to check
our own reference against. Section~\\ref{sec:refcheck} reports that comparison.

This paper addresses the three limitations in turn and extends the evaluation
to the excluded regime. The principal findings are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Reproducing the advection case over 20 seeds and then measuring the
    trained factors --- which the source paper does not report --- shows that
    the stated mechanism does not survive training. At the published $\wU$ the
    trained $U$ is rank-deficient rather than near-orthogonal, and
    $\mathrm{diag}(S)$ is not the effective weight's spectrum. The accuracy
    gain is real; the explanation for it is not
    (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
  \item Removing the penalty by constraining $U$ to be exactly orthogonal is
    stable but recovers only about half the gain, and replacing it with a
    bound on $U$'s largest singular value --- the weaker constraint that
    reading suggests --- is the worst regime we tried
    (Section~\ref{sec:bounded}).
  \item On shock-dominated shallow-water dam breaks no spectral regime
    separates from an unmodified network by more than about 10\% in integrated
    depth error, and $\wU$ can be moved three decades without measurable
    effect, so the tuning burden of the first limitation does not arise here
    (Sections~\ref{sec:swe}, \ref{sec:sweep}). On the one smooth case in the
    set, the constrained regimes are a factor of 2.5 \emph{worse} than an
    unmodified network.
  \item The overhead of the second limitation does not reproduce: the penalty
    costs 3\%, while the penalty-free alternative that would replace it costs
    42\%, so eliminating $\wU$ costs time rather than saving it
    (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
  \item Establishing any of this required checking our finite-volume reference
    against an independently implemented solver family. On two of three
    benchmarks the two disagree by more than the differences the comparison is
    trying to resolve (Section~\ref{sec:refcheck}). We report this at length
    because a single fine-grid run is the usual reference in this literature
    and its uncertainty is rarely quoted.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Where the failure is located.}
The three accounts sketched in the introduction --- architecture, loss balance,
weight structure --- are not exclusive, and the same benchmark often improves
under all three. That makes attribution hard and is part of why this paper
spends its effort on measurement rather than on a new variant. The
architectural line treats the network's frequency response as the binding
constraint and modifies the input encoding or the
activation~\cite{tancik2020fourier, sitzmann2020siren, jagtap2020adaptive};
we include a Fourier-feature network as a baseline throughout, because it is the
standard remedy for exactly the steep targets~\cite{wang2026cnpinn} opens with
and it is absent from that paper's comparison. A sine activation appears there
only as an ablation of their own method, not as a SIREN baseline.

\paragraph{Loss balance and constraint handling.}
The loss-weighting line rebalances terms whose gradients differ in
magnitude~\cite{wang2021gradient, wang2022ntk, mcclenny2023selfadaptive}.
AL-PINNs~\cite{son2023alpinn} instead treat the initial and boundary data as
constraints and apply an augmented-Lagrangian method, replacing a fixed weight
by a multiplier with an update rule. The distinction from the present work is
what the constraint is placed on: those methods constrain the \emph{solution}
the network represents, whereas the penalty studied here constrains the
\emph{weights}. The distinction matters concretely, because it explains the
failure~\cite{wang2026cnpinn} report in their Section 4.6.3: adaptive schemes
from this family diverge on their penalty because it begins at exactly zero, a
pathology specific to a constraint that is satisfied at initialisation by
construction.

\paragraph{Spectral structure in the weights.}
Spectral normalisation~\cite{miyato2018spectral} divides each weight by its
largest singular value to enforce a 1-Lipschitz map. It acts on the same object
as the method studied here, with the opposite intent: it \emph{caps} the
gradient magnitude the network can express, whereas the diagnostic motivating
cnPINN holds that networks fail on steep targets by expressing too little of
it. Our bounded regime (Section~\ref{sec:bounded}) is close to spectral
normalisation in form, and the fact that it is the worst regime we tried is
consistent with the two having opposite aims. Exactly-orthogonal weights are
available through the matrix-exponential and Cayley parametrisations of
Lezcano-Casado and Mart\'inez-Rubio~\cite{lezcano2019orthogonal}, introduced
for recurrent networks where the motivation is gradient stability over long
sequences; we are not aware of a prior application to PINN training.
SVD-PINNs~\cite{gao2022svdpinn} use the same three-factor layer as our
spectrum-only regime, freezing both orthogonal factors and retraining the
singular values, but for transfer between equations rather than from-scratch
training. The construction is theirs; what we add is its behaviour as a
from-scratch method, which is poor.

\paragraph{PINNs for hyperbolic problems and shocks.}
A pointwise PDE residual is not defined at a discontinuity, so the line of work
aimed at conservation laws changes the objective or the architecture rather
than the weights. Weak and entropy formulations replace the strong residual
with one that remains defined for discontinuous solutions and select the
physically admissible weak solution~\cite{deryck2024wpinn}; other approaches
impose jump conditions through the architecture, concentrate loss weight near
detected shocks, or borrow relaxation formulations from the numerical-analysis
literature. None of these measures or controls a property of the network's
weights, so the comparison reported here is orthogonal to them rather than
competing with them. Composing a spectral parametrisation with a weak-form loss
is untested, and we do not test it: our finite-volume-informed baseline is a
different composition, in which the residual is that of a discrete conservative
update rather than of the differential equation.

\paragraph{Shallow-water dam breaks.}
PINNs have been applied to the shallow-water equations for flood and dam-break
prediction, generally on smooth or mildly discontinuous cases, and hybrids that
embed a finite-volume update in the loss are the closest relatives of our
physics-only baseline. That literature is surveyed in the companion study
of~\cite{dambreak2026comparative}, from which our benchmark definitions,
conventions ($[0,100]^2$~m domain, $g = 2$~m/s$^2$, Gaussian-hump bed,
reflective walls) and differentiable HLLC solver are taken. Two of that study's
results carry directly into this one. Its finding that the
finite-volume-informed formulation collapses on short dam-break windows is
reproduced here as a baseline rather than reinvestigated
(Section~\ref{sec:swe}). Its reference solver output --- HLL, Lax--Wendroff and
MUSCL--Rusanov runs on a $501^2$ node grid for the same initial conditions,
produced by an implementation independent of ours --- is what makes
Section~\ref{sec:refcheck} possible; without a second implementation there
would be no way to put an uncertainty on the reference at all. Reusing a
companion study's apparatus buys a controlled comparison and costs
independence, and we flag both.

\paragraph{Reference practice.}
We found no comparable check in the PINN-for-conservation-laws literature. The
standard is to compare against one fine-grid run of one scheme, or against an
analytical solution where one exists, and to report neural errors to two or
three significant figures without an uncertainty on the reference itself.
Section~\ref{sec:refcheck} shows that on two of our three checkable benchmarks
that uncertainty exceeds the differences being reported, which suggests the
practice is worth revisiting beyond this paper.

\paragraph{What we did not find.}
A search in August 2026 returned no PINN work training weights under an exact
orthogonality constraint, no condition-number or gradient-norm mismatch
diagnostic for PINNs on hyperbolic problems, and no indexed citation
of~\cite{wang2026cnpinn}, which had appeared online two months earlier. The
searches covered arXiv-indexed material and are not exhaustive --- in
particular, a method published under different terminology for the same
construction would not necessarily surface --- so the sweep needs repeating
before submission.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The factorised layer}

Write a hidden layer's weight as $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$. At
initialisation, and only there, it is factorised by singular value
decomposition as
\begin{equation}
  W_0 = U_0 S_0 V_0^\top, \qquad
  U_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times r},\;
  S_0 = \mathrm{diag}(s_0),\;
  V_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times r},
  \label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
with $r = \min(m,n)$. Thereafter the layer's effective weight is rebuilt from
the stored factors on every forward pass, $W = U\,\mathrm{diag}(s)\,V^\top$,
and the decomposition is never recomputed during training. Following the source
paper, $V$ stays frozen at $V_0$, the first and last layers of the network keep
ordinary dense weights, and only the square hidden-to-hidden weights are
factorised. The parameter count rises by $r$ per factorised layer.

If $U$ were orthogonal, the singular values of $W$ would be exactly $|s|$, and
the optimiser would have direct control of each layer's spectrum. That is the
mechanism the method is built on, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:penalty} is what is
supposed to keep $U$ close enough to orthogonal for it to hold. Whether it does
is the question of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.

\subsection{Weight parameterisations compared}

Five parameterisations of the hidden layers are compared. They share
Eq.~\eqref{eq:factor} and keep $V = V_0$ frozen, and differ in what is trained
and in what constrains $U$. Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic} shows the construction and
the admissible spectrum of $U$ in each case; Table~\ref{tab:regimes}
summarises them.

In the unconstrained case the weight $W$ is trained directly, without
factorisation; this is the vanilla network and serves as the baseline. The
penalised parameterisation trains $U$ and $s$ and adds
$\wU \sum_j \DU_j^2$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:penalty} to the loss, which is the
method of~\cite{wang2026cnpinn} and carries the single free parameter $\wU$.

The exactly orthogonal parameterisation confines $U$ to the Stiefel manifold
using the matrix-exponential map of Lezcano-Casado and
Mart\'inez-Rubio~\cite{lezcano2019orthogonal}, so that $U^\top U = I$ holds to
machine precision after every optimiser step and the singular values of $W$
equal $|s|$ exactly. The penalty term is then redundant and $\wU$ does not
appear. This parameterisation and the penalised one target the same property
by different means, one approximately with a tuned weight and one exactly
without, so a comparison between them isolates the contribution of the penalty
from the contribution of the factorisation.

In the spectrum-only parameterisation both $U$ and $V$ are frozen at their
orthogonal initial values and $s$ alone is trained, giving $r$ trainable
parameters per layer in place of $mn$ and exact spectral control by
construction. The same three-factor layer is used by SVD-PINNs, where the
singular vectors of a trained network are retained and the singular values
retrained for a new equation~\cite{gao2022svdpinn}; the construction is not
introduced here, and the difference is that training proceeds from scratch.

The fifth parameterisation constrains only the largest singular value of $U$,
rescaling it on every forward pass so that $\sigma_{\max}(U) = 1$ while the
remaining singular values stay free in $[0,1]$. It is therefore strictly weaker
than exact orthogonality, which fixes all of them at unity, and it requires no
penalty. It is introduced in Section~\ref{sec:bounded}, where the factor
measurements of Section~\ref{sec:diag} motivate it.

\subsection{Implementation}

The penalty of Eq.~\eqref{eq:penalty} uses the matrix 2-norm, following the
source paper. An early version of our code used the Frobenius norm, which
upper-bounds it by up to $\sqrt{n}$ and so is up to 128 times stronger at width
128. Both are reported rather than one being discarded: the dam-break matrix of
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} was produced with the Frobenius penalty and the sweep of
Table~\ref{tab:sweep} with Eq.~\eqref{eq:penalty}, and
Section~\ref{sec:sweep} compares them on the same benchmarks. Only the
penalised entry reads that norm, so no other row is affected by the change.

The advection case follows the source paper's protocol: L-BFGS with a
strong-Wolfe line search, 3000 iterations. The shallow-water cases use Adam at
$10^{-3}$ for 20000 iterations, since L-BFGS on a two-dimensional
time-dependent problem with 20000 resampled collocation points is not
practical. One regime forces a departure from this: the singular-value box of
Section~\ref{sec:bounded} cannot be trained by L-BFGS at all, for reasons given
there, so where it appears the optimiser is stated explicitly and its numbers
are not compared across protocols.

Random seeds are set before model construction rather than before the training
loop, so initial weights --- and, where used, the Fourier feature matrix --- are
reproducible from the seed alone. Every reported cell is five seeds on the
shallow-water benchmarks and twenty on advection.

\subsection{Factor geometry after training}
\label{sec:diag}

The source paper reports errors, not factor geometry. We measure, per
factorised layer of trained networks over 3 seeds, the orthogonality defect
$\DU = \|U^\top U - I\|_2$ and the largest relative gap between the trained
$|s|$ and the singular values of the effective weight $USV^\top$
(Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic}).

At the published weight $\DU \approx 1$, which for a defect measured in the
2-norm means an eigendirection of $U^\top U$ has collapsed: the trained factor
is rank-deficient, not near-orthogonal. The trained $s$ is not the effective
weight's spectrum. Both premises of the stated mechanism fail, while the
method's accuracy advantage holds.

Ordering the regimes by median error gives unconstrained-parameterised
$2.1\times10^{-1}$, spectrum-only $1.2\times10^{-1}$, unconstrained
$8.4\times10^{-2}$, exactly orthogonal $1.7\times10^{-2}$, weakly penalised
$8.4\times10^{-3}$. The penalty is doing something the method needs: removing
it lets the defect grow by two orders of magnitude and destroys the gain, while
enforcing the constraint it nominally approximates recovers only about half of
it. Exact orthogonality is a stronger constraint than the problem wants. The
natural reading is that the term bounds the scale of $U$ rather than
orthogonalising it, and Section~\ref{sec:bounded} tests that reading
directly --- it does not survive.

This reframes the first stated limitation: the open question is not how to
adapt $\wU$ but which constraint class the term belongs to. The ordering above
suggested that a bound weaker than orthogonality --- a cap on $U$'s largest
singular value, or a box on all of them --- should recover the gain without a
tuned penalty. The first of these is evaluated below.

\subsection{A weaker constraint on the left factor}
\label{sec:bounded}

The bounded regime rescales $U$ on every forward pass so that
$\sigma_{\max}(U) = 1$, leaving the remaining singular values free in $[0,1]$.
It is strictly weaker than orthogonality, which fixes all of them at 1, and it
admits the rank-deficient factor the penalty was measured to produce. It
carries no penalty term and no $\wU$. The scale it removes is not lost to the
layer, since $W = U\,\mathrm{diag}(s)\,V^\top$ and $s$ is trained.

Over 20 seeds it reaches $(4.68\pm5.1)\times10^{-1}$ mean and
$3.7\times10^{-1}$ median relative error: the worst of the six regimes, below
even the unpenalised parameterisation. The prediction is refuted.

Measuring the bounded networks' factors explains the failure and corrects our
earlier reading (last row of Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic}). The bounded regime
reaches $\DU = 1.00$, essentially the same defect as the penalised regime's
$0.98$ --- so on the statistic we used to characterise the penalty, the two are
indistinguishable. They are not indistinguishable in behaviour: the gap between
the trained $|s|$ and the effective weight's singular values is $13\times$ under
the penalty and $1290\times$ under the cap, and the bounded networks drive their
smallest trained singular value to $0$ exactly.

The reason is that $\DU = \max_i |\sigma_i(U)^2 - 1|$ saturates as soon as a
single direction of $U$ collapses, so it cannot distinguish one collapsed
direction from many. Capping $\sigma_{\max}(U)$ reproduces the value of $\DU$
while leaving the rest of the spectrum free to collapse, and the effective
weight then has nothing to do with the spectrum $s$ nominally controls. The
conclusion is narrower than the one we drew from the accuracy ordering alone:
what the penalty supplies is not a bound on the factor's scale --- a bound on
scale is exactly what the cap provides, and it fails --- and $\DU$ is too coarse
a summary to say what it is instead. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}'s reading was
underdetermined by its own evidence.

The other half of the prediction is a box holding every singular value of $U$
in $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$: the constraint class that matches the
measured behaviour, with $\varepsilon$ a geometric knob that recovers exact
orthogonality at $\varepsilon = 0$ and the unconstrained parameterisation as
$\varepsilon$ grows, in place of a loss weight on an unnormalised penalty. We
implemented it as a projection, which proved untrainable under the present
protocol. The projection itself is exact, in that trained networks have every
singular value inside the box to five decimal places, but its Jacobian is
ill-conditioned precisely where the layer operates: differentiating through the singular value decomposition introduces
$1/(\sigma_i^2 - \sigma_j^2)$ terms, and the box holds every $\sigma_i$ near 1.
The exact gradient produced non-finite values within a few hundred iterations.
A straight-through estimator is stable and keeps the constraint, but its
gradient disagrees with the function value, and L-BFGS with a strong-Wolfe line
search then makes almost no progress: over 300 iterations the loss falls from
$2.91\times10^{-1}$ to $2.76\times10^{-1}$, against $1.82\times10^{-1}$ for the
exactly-orthogonal regime from the same initialisation. This is therefore an open implementation problem, and does not constitute
evidence about the constraint class itself.

The difficulty is itself relevant to the paper's subject. A soft penalty is
differentiable everywhere and costs one matrix norm per layer; the hard
constraint it nominally approximates is a projection whose exact derivative is
singular on the set where the constraint binds. That asymmetry is a practical
reason for the source paper's design that its own discussion does not give, and
it is a better argument for the penalty than the mechanism the paper offers.
Whether a first-order optimiser tolerates the straight-through estimator well
enough to make the box trainable remains untested.

The evidence here is one smooth one-dimensional problem, one architecture and
one optimiser. Whether the ordering survives on a shock is a separate
question, which is what Section~\ref{sec:swe} asks.

\section{Shock-dominated shallow-water benchmarks}
\label{sec:swe}

\subsection{Benchmarks and evaluation protocol}

The benchmarks are two-dimensional dam breaks over a Gaussian-hump bed at the
conventions of the comparative study of Zahra
et al.~\cite{dambreak2026comparative}: domain $[0,100]^2$~m,
$g = 2$~m/s$^2$, reflective walls on all four sides, $t_{\text{end}} = 2$~s.
We use a step release, a circular column over a wet bed, the same column over
a dry bed, and a smooth Gaussian mound, all with Manning $n = 0$. A fifth
case, three submerged humps with Manning friction over 20~s, is included and
then set aside for the reason given below. Errors are
$L^1$ and $L^2$ of depth at the final output time on a shared $128^2$
evaluation grid, against a reference computed in-process with an HLLC
MUSCL--van Leer finite-volume scheme~\cite{toro2009riemann} at $512^2$ with
hydrostatic reconstruction for well-balancing~\cite{audusse2004wellbalanced}.
Baselines are an unmodified network, a Fourier-feature network --- the
standard remedy for high-frequency targets, absent from the source paper's
comparison --- and a finite-volume-informed network. Five seeds per cell.

Every strong-form entry is a 6-layer, 128-unit tanh network mapping
$(x,y,t)$ to primitive variables $(h,u,v)$, trained by Adam at $10^{-3}$ for
20000 iterations with gradient clipping at 1.0, on 20000 collocation points
resampled each step, with the initial condition imposed as a penalty at every
cell centre weighted 10 against the residual. Following the source paper, the
input and output layers stay dense and only the square hidden weights are
factorised. The Fourier-feature baseline uses 32 frequencies. The
finite-volume-informed entry predicts cell averages on the solver grid at 41
collocation times and is trained against the residual of the discrete SSP-RK2
update.

\subsection{Accuracy across benchmarks}

Table~\ref{tab:matrix} gives all five benchmarks, five seeds each. On the
three shock-dominated cases no spectral regime separates from an unmodified
network by more than about 10\% in $L^1$ depth: on the step release every
entry but the finite-volume-informed one falls between $1.03$ and
$1.13\times10^3$, inside the unconstrained baseline's own seed spread. The
unconstrained network sits a factor of $4.1$ to $4.4$ above a classical scheme
at the same resolution.

Fig.~\ref{fig:shock} resolves that summary on the circular dam break. The
reference at $t = 2$~s consists of an outward-travelling bore near $x = 22$~m
and $x = 78$~m, a rarefaction behind it, and a plateau whose crest carries a
shallow depression left by the Gaussian bed. Panels (B)--(D) show that all
three parameterisations reproduce the gross structure; panels (E) and (F) show
where they do not. Two error modes dominate and are common to all three. The
bore is displaced and smeared, producing deviations of $-2$~m at the two front
positions against $-1.0$~m for a classical scheme at the evaluation resolution.
Behind it the networks return a flat crest near $10.2$~m where the reference
has a depression near $9.6$~m, giving a broad $+1.3$~m deviation across
$40 < x < 60$~m. The classical scheme at $128^2$ captures the depression and
deviates only at the fronts.

Neither error mode responds to the parameterisation. The three curves in panel
(F) lie within a few centimetres of one another over most of the domain and
differ visibly only in the immediate neighbourhood of the bore. What limits a
strong-form network on this problem is therefore not a property that the weight
spectrum controls.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the penalty weight}
\label{sec:sweep}

The first of the source paper's stated limitations is that $\wU$ must be tuned
per equation, and no published value exists for shallow-water flow. We
bracketed it over three decades on the two circular benchmarks with the
corrected Eq.~\eqref{eq:penalty} penalty, five seeds per cell
(Table~\ref{tab:sweep}).

Three orders of magnitude in $\wU$ move the error by 3\% on the wet bed and

knob does not do anything. That is a negative answer to their first
limitation in this regime rather than a solution to it: there is nothing to
adapt. It also says the penalty is not the active ingredient here, which is
consistent with the whole spectral family sitting within 10\% of an
unmodified network on the same benchmarks.

This sweep supersedes one reading we drew from the earlier, Frobenius-penalty
run and now retract. There, the penalised network placed the radial front on
the dry bed at $3.5\pm4.1$~m from the reference against the unconstrained
network's $21.2\pm8.6$~m, which we took as a local advantage invisible to the
integrated error. Under the corrected penalty the same quantity is
$14.8\pm6.6$~m at $\wU = 1/35000$ and between $14.8$ and $17.0$~m across the
three decades, overlapping the unconstrained baseline within one standard
deviation. The apparent front-position advantage belonged to the stronger
Frobenius penalty, not to the published method, and it does not survive.
Integrated depth error was almost unaffected by the same change
($1.662\times10^3$ against $1.601\times10^3$ on the wet bed), so the norm
choice matters for where the shock is placed and not for the depth field as a
whole.

The overhead the source paper reports does not reproduce in this setting: the

The direction of the comparison matters more than the absolute numbers.
Removing the penalty by constraining the factor exactly costs 42\%, because
the matrix-exponential retraction runs on every forward pass. So on cost
grounds the penalty-free route is worse than the penalty, not better, and the
motivation for eliminating $\wU$ has to rest on the tuning burden alone ---
which Section~\ref{sec:sweep} shows does not exist on these problems either.
Spectrum-only training is free relative to the baseline, as expected from
training $n$ parameters per layer instead of $n^2$, but it buys nothing in
accuracy.

Lax--Wendroff is the outlier throughout, which is expected of Lax--Wendroff

rather than about ours. Nothing here says our reference is wrong; it is the

confirmed. What it says is that a comparison table of this kind has a
resolution floor that varies by benchmark, and that the floor has to be
quoted next to any margin claimed from it. We have not seen this check
performed in the PINN-for-conservation-laws literature, where a single
fine-grid run is the usual reference.

\section{Limitations}

Five seeds per cell resolve a factor of two, not the 10\% differences that
make up most of Table~\ref{tab:matrix}; only the Gaussian-mound column
separates by more than the spread. Section~\ref{sec:refcheck} bounds what the
comparison can resolve at all and should be read before
Table~\ref{tab:matrix}, not after it. The three-hump case is reported but not
interpretable: every model there loses most of its mass.

The factor-geometry measurement of Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic} uses 3 seeds on
one one-dimensional problem, one architecture and one optimiser, and it is the
basis for the paper's central claim about what the penalty does. It should be
repeated on the shallow-water networks, where the per-run factor diagnostics
are now recorded but not yet analysed.

The singular-value box of Section~\ref{sec:bounded}, which is what the refuted
prediction points to, is not implemented. Until it is, the claim that the
penalty acts by pulling the whole spectrum toward 1 rests on one measurement
of $D$ and one failed alternative, both on the advection case.

Two of the five contributions in the project proposal have no section here.
The pointwise condition-number field, which is the diagnostic that would
explain \emph{where} on the shock the spectral variants fail rather than only
that they do, is not built. Neither is the Navier--Stokes leg --- the
Taylor--Green case the source paper reports and the lid-driven cavity --- which
is what would connect these results back to the setting~\cite{wang2026cnpinn}
validates in. Cost and memory were recorded per run but are not tabulated, so
the second stated limitation of the source paper is not yet answered
quantitatively.

\section{Conclusion}

The orthogonality penalty of~\cite{wang2026cnpinn} is load-bearing on their
own advection problem and is not doing what the paper says it does. At the
published weight the trained factor is rank-deficient rather than orthogonal,
and the trained singular values are not the effective weight's spectrum, yet
removing the penalty destroys the accuracy gain and enforcing exact
orthogonality recovers only about half of it. Replacing the penalty with a
bound on the factor's largest singular value --- the weaker constraint that
reading suggests --- is the worst of the six regimes we tried. That rules the
scale-bound reading out rather than replacing it: the bounded networks match
the penalised ones on the orthogonality defect $\DU$ while missing the
effective spectrum by two further orders of magnitude, so $\DU$ is too coarse
a statistic to characterise what the penalty does, and what it does instead
remains open.

Moving to shock-dominated shallow-water flow, outside the reach of the source
paper's Theorem 2.1, the picture is flatter and in one place reversed. On the
three shock cases no spectral regime separates from an unmodified network by
more than about 10\% in integrated depth error, and the penalty weight can be
moved three decades without measurable effect, so the tuning problem the
source paper poses does not arise. On the one smooth case in the set, exact
orthogonality and spectrum-only training are a factor of $2.5$ \emph{worse}
than an unmodified network, by a margin that exceeds the reference
uncertainty. Whatever the reparameterisation contributes on smooth
one-dimensional targets does not transfer, and constraining the factor
hurts most where the target is smoothest.

Establishing any of this required checking the reference against an
independent solver family, which on two of three benchmarks disagrees with it
by more than the differences under test. We would encourage that check as
routine practice: a single fine-grid run is the usual reference in this
literature, and its uncertainty is rarely quoted.
